Asset update

What targeted capital work actually fixes

Roofs, boilers, windows and units. The least interesting work in real estate is the work that decides whether a building performs.

Published 24 November 2026 by Fairbanks Commercial Properties

There is a version of property investment that happens in spreadsheets, and there is the version that happens in a mechanical room in February. The second one is what moves the numbers.

Start with what the building needs, not what shows

When we take on a legacy building, the temptation is to spend first on what a prospective tenant sees: paint, flooring, a new sign. We do the opposite, because the things that drive a tenant out are rarely cosmetic. They are heat that fails, water that does not run hot, a window that leaks cold air across a living room, a common area that feels unsafe at night.

So the order is systems first, then the unit interiors, then common areas. It costs more up front and it shows less, but it is the difference between a tenant who renews and a tenant who serves notice in March.

Do the work before the unit goes back on the market

A unit that is re-let in poor condition sets the rent for the next several years and brings its problems with it. Doing the work first takes the unit out of service for longer, which looks worse in a monthly report, and then produces a unit that fills faster and holds.

We see that directly in our own turnover. Buildings that arrived with no meaningful benchmark for how quickly a vacant apartment could be re-let now fill a renovated unit in a matter of weeks.

What it does to operating performance

Capital work is usually discussed as a cost. In a building that has been under-invested in, it is better understood as the thing that unlocks the income already available in the asset.

On the assets we have taken all the way through this process, operating income has improved by a wide margin against where it sat when we bought. The buildings did not change location and the market did not transform. What changed was the condition of the units and how the building is run.

On the larger buildings still in progress, the gap between the operating margin we inherited and the one we are working toward is substantial. That gap is what disciplined capital work and in house management are for. It is a target rather than a result, and the work is still going on.

The part that does not finish

Capital work is not a project with an end date. A building that has been brought back will need the same attention again, on a longer cycle. Owners who treat the renovation as a one off and then stop spending end up exactly where the previous owner was, which is how these buildings became available to us in the first place.
