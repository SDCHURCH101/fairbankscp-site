Why Fairbanks rents hold up when new building stalls

Interior Alaska has a short construction season and a long list of reasons new housing does not get built. That shapes what happens to the buildings already standing.

Published 24 November 2026 by Fairbanks Commercial Properties

People who look at Fairbanks from outside tend to assume the rental market behaves like a Lower 48 city of a similar size. It does not, and the reason is supply.

New supply arrives slowly, or not at all

Building here is constrained in ways that have nothing to do with demand. The season you can pour concrete and frame in is short. Materials come up the Dalton Highway by truck or in by barge and aircraft, and they cost what that journey implies. Labor has to be found, housed and kept through a winter. By the time a developer has worked through all of that, the rent a new building would need to charge is well above what the market is paying.

The result is that very little new rental stock gets added in any given year. When demand moves, supply cannot move with it. That is the single most important fact about this market.

Demand does not rest on one employer

Fairbanks has several demand sources that do not move together. There is a substantial military presence. There is the University of Alaska. There is healthcare, and there are the trades that serve the Interior and the North Slope. A single closure or a bad year in one sector does not empty the town the way it can in a place built around one industry.

That combination, constrained supply and a demand base with several legs, is what makes occupancy in good buildings here more stable than people expect.

What it means for an existing building

If nobody can economically add new units, the competitive set for a well run apartment is not a new development down the road. It is the other existing buildings, most of which have deferred maintenance of their own. A unit that has been properly brought back is competing against tired stock rather than against something newer and better.

We see this in our own buildings. Where we have taken a tired property and actually fixed the units, occupancy has followed, vacancies have come down steadily, and a renovated unit now re-lets in a fraction of the time it used to take. The constraint was never demand. It was condition.

The demand was always there. What was missing was somewhere worth renting.

The limits of the argument

None of this makes a building safe on its own. Constrained supply protects an owner who maintains the asset and runs it properly. It does nothing for an owner who does not, because a tired unit in a tight market is still a tired unit. And conditions change: a large employer could expand or contract, and construction costs could fall. What is written here describes the market as we find it, not a forecast.
