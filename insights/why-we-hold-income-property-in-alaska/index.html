Why we hold income property in Alaska instead of trading it

Long term ownership, downside protection, and why operational control matters more here than it does in a liquid market.

Published 24 November 2026 by Fairbanks Commercial Properties

Our holding period is measured in years and decades. That is a deliberate choice, and in Alaska it is close to a necessary one.

The exit is not reliable, so do not depend on it

In a deep market you can underwrite a sale in year five with some confidence that a buyer will be there. Interior Alaska is not a deep market. The pool of buyers for a 177 unit building in downtown Fairbanks is small, and it is smallest exactly when you would most want to sell.

If the exit cannot be relied on, the return has to come from somewhere else, and that leaves rent. Once you accept that, a great deal of your behavior changes. You stop deferring the roof to protect a disposal date. You take the tenant who will still be there in four years over the one who will pay more for one.

An owner who cannot sell easily is forced to become a good operator. That turns out to be an advantage.

Operational control is the whole thing

We do not subcontract the parts of this business that determine whether a building works. Diligence, capital decisions, leasing and day to day operations all sit with the same team, in the same office, in the same town as the buildings.

Third party management is a reasonable model in a city with a deep bench of managers competing for the work. Here, handing a building to an outside manager means the person deciding whether a boiler gets replaced this year has no stake in whether it fails in three. We would rather carry that ourselves.

What protects the downside

Three things, in our experience. Supply that cannot respond quickly, which supports occupancy in maintained buildings. A demand base with several independent legs, so no single employer dictates the market. And a mix of asset types across the portfolio: roughly 400,000 square feet spanning 201 homes in Fairbanks, office and industrial space, and 234 bedrooms of crew accommodation on the North Slope. Fairbanks apartments and Slope industrial follow different economics and rarely go soft at the same time.

What this is not

It is not a claim that Alaska real estate cannot lose money. Buildings here are expensive to heat and expensive to repair, the labor pool is thin, and a single major employer changing course would be felt across the whole Interior. Long term ownership does not remove those risks. It changes which of them you are equipped to manage, and it gives you time, which is usually the thing a struggling asset needs most.

Nothing here is an offer to sell or a solicitation to buy any security, and past results on our assets describe what has happened rather than what will.
