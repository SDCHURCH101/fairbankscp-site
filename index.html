How we work

What we commit to, on every building

We take on buildings others have given up on

Interior Alaska has sound structures in locations that still matter, sitting under years of deferred maintenance. Those are the ones we go after. Bringing one back puts housing and workspace into service that the community would otherwise lose.

We put real capital into them

Roofs, boilers, windows, whole units. The work happens on our own account before the space goes back on the market, because people deserve somewhere that has actually been fixed rather than painted over.

We answer for them ourselves

Leasing, tenant relations, maintenance and vendors all sit with our own team at 300 Barnette Street. When a heater fails at two in the morning in February, the person who picks up works for the owner.

We stay

Our return comes from rent collected year after year, not from selling the building on. That lets us make decisions a short term owner cannot, and it means our tenants are not meeting a new landlord every few years.

Why Interior Alaska

Why we keep putting money into this region

We are not diversified across the Lower 48 and we are not trying to be. This is the market we know, and these are the reasons we are still buying in it.

Very little gets built here

New construction in Interior Alaska is slow, costly and limited by the weather. That puts a floor under demand for existing buildings that have been properly looked after.

Infrastructure is moving

Energy, transportation and regional development work across the Interior could lift economic activity over the long run. Demand for homes and workspace tends to follow it.

Demand does not rest on one employer

The military bases, the University of Alaska, healthcare and the trades all draw people to Fairbanks. No single closure takes the whole market down with it.

Real buildings hold up

Well located property in a market where supply cannot react quickly has tended to hold its value through inflation better than most things a person can own.

Where we operate

Three communities, one team

Fairbanks

The second largest city in Alaska and the service center for everything north of the Alaska Range. Most of our apartments are here, including income qualified homes for households the open market prices out.

North Pole

Ten miles southeast of Fairbanks on the highway, where local contractors and tradespeople can get yard space alongside a building instead of hunting for storage across town.

Prudhoe Bay and Deadhorse

Industrial buildings, hangars and crew accommodation on the North Slope. Everything up here came up the Dalton Highway by truck, or in by barge and aircraft, so keeping what already stands in service is the only practical way to house and supply the people working the field.
